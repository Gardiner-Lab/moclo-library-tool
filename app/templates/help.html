How to use the MoClo Library Tool

Golden Gate cloning works because everyone's parts share the same fusion sites. This page covers the levels, getting a part into the library properly, checking that an assembly will work, planning one, the reaction calculator, the CRISPR designer, and what to do when a part will not fit. Golden rules

  1. The fusion sites are the contract. A part is only usable if its 4 bp overhangs are the standard ones for its position. Everything else about a part can be fixed later; these cannot.
  2. No internal sites. A Level 0 part must not contain BsaI, and a Level 1 plasmid must not contain BpiI, or the enzyme will cut the thing you are trying to build. The tool checks, but domesticate before you order the synthesis, not after.
  3. Annotate before you upload. A part whose promoter, CDS and terminator are marked is usable by the whole lab; a bare sequence is a mystery in six months.
  4. Say where it came from. The lab source and contributor are how someone finds the tube, and how credit stays with the person who made it.
  5. Check compatibility before you order anything. The checker takes a minute and finds the clash that would otherwise appear as an empty plate.
  6. Save the plan to your dashboard. The reaction, its parts and its volumes are then there when you come to do it at the bench, and again when it needs repeating.

The levels

Golden Gate assembly goes up in levels, each cut by a different enzyme so the level below is never disturbed:

Level 0 parts— BsaI → Level 1 transcription unit— BpiI → Level 2 multigene plasmid
  • Level 0 is a single element: a promoter, a coding sequence, a terminator, an intron, or something else. It lives in the Parts tab.
  • Level 1 joins those into one working transcription unit — promoter, CDS, terminator — which the tool keeps as a cassette.
  • Level 2 joins several transcription units into one plasmid, under Plasmids.
  • Backbones are the acceptor vectors each level assembles into.

Adding a part

  1. Upload the GenBank file, or use Parts → add and paste the sequence.
  2. Give it a clear name and the right type: coding, promoter, terminator, intron, other, or an expression cassette for a Level 1 unit.
  3. Set the 5′ and 3′ overhangs — the 4 bp fusion sites. These decide where the part can sit in an assembly, so take them from the standard for its position rather than from the sequence by eye.
  4. Fill in the lab source and contributor, so the tube can be found and the work credited.
  5. Save. The part is then in the lab's shared library and can be used by anyone.

A plasmid in the Plasmid Editor can be ticked Golden Gate ready and contributed straight to this library; the editor first checks that one enzyme releases a single clean insert with two fusion sites.

Annotating before you upload

What a part's GenBank file should carry, and how to mark it, is set out in detail in the annotation guide. In short: mark the real feature boundaries, use consistent names and types, and leave out the vector the part arrived in.

Checking compatibility

Before ordering or setting up a reaction, have the tool check the set of parts you mean to use. It looks for the two things that actually stop an assembly:

  • Overhang clashes — two parts offering the same fusion site, so the order is ambiguous — and gaps, where nothing joins one part to the next.
  • Internal enzyme sites — a BsaI site inside a Level 0 part, or a BpiI site inside a Level 1 plasmid, which the reaction would cut.

It tells you which part is at fault and why, rather than only that the set will not work.

Planning an assembly

  1. Assembly: choose the level you are building, and the backbone to assemble into.
  2. Add the parts in the order they go: promoter, CDS, terminator for a Level 1; the transcription units for a Level 2.
  3. The tool joins them through their fusion sites and shows the assembled plasmid with its parts marked, so you can see what you are about to make.
  4. Save it under Plasmids, where it keeps the parts it was made from.
  5. Send it to the reaction calculator to work out the mix.

The reaction calculator

Under Protocol. Give it the fragments and their sizes, or send a plasmid to it from its page, and it works out how much of each to use for an equimolar reaction, with the enzyme, buffer and ligase, scaled to the number of reactions you are setting up.

The same mix can be put straight into a Lab Notebook day page as a table, so what you pipetted is recorded where the rest of the day is.

The CRISPR designer

  1. CRISPR Designer: give the target sequence and how many guides you want.
  2. It designs the guides and the oligos for them, and plans the whole route: the Level −1 guide modules, the Level 0 parts, the Level 1 plasmid and the Level 2 construct.
  3. Name the records it will make, then let it make them. The parts and plasmids appear in the library like any others.
  4. Save to dashboard keeps the whole protocol, so it can be read again later without redesigning it.

Saving what you planned

Save to dashboard, on a plasmid or at the end of the CRISPR designer, keeps it under Saved constructs on your dashboard, with the reactions it needs.

  • Its title, and Open plasmid ↗, open the plasmid itself — or the cassette, for a single transcription unit.
  • Each reaction opens the calculator already filled in, which is how you pick it up again at the bench.
  • View protocol, for a CRISPR construct, regenerates the whole protocol as it was.

The editor and the rest of the suite

  • Any part, backbone or plasmid opens in the Plasmid Editor for editing, restriction analysis or read alignment: both tools share one library.
  • A plasmid confirmed by sequencing in the editor gets its glycerol stock in Lab Stocks.
  • A reaction can be put into a Lab Notebook page, with its parts and volumes.
  • Export a part or plasmid as GenBank, FASTA, SnapGene or an image.

Fixing mistakes

  • A part will not go into an assembly: check its overhangs first. They are the usual cause, and they are easy to mistype.
  • The checker reports an internal site: the part must be domesticated — the site changed silently — before it can be used at that level.
  • Wrong part type or source: edit the part. Its sequence and overhangs are what others depend on; the rest is free to correct.
  • Assembled the wrong thing: delete the plasmid and assemble again. The parts it used are untouched.
  • A part is in the library twice: keep the annotated one and delete the other, after checking nothing was assembled from it.
  • Not sure a saved construct is still right: open it from the dashboard — if its plasmid has been deleted, the row says so rather than giving a broken link.
